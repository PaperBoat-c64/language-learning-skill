# The Community Garden

**Language**: English → English (monolingual study) | **Genre**: short narrative | **Mode**: study | **Level**: unspecified; default-adaptive glosses for a B1 reader

> The source is already in English, so this version paraphrases rather than translates. The original sentences remain the object of study and are quoted verbatim.

## Plain-English paraphrase

Last spring, our neighborhood association decided to turn an empty, unused lot into a garden for the community. We put together a few grants in a rough, improvised way, borrowed tools from anyone willing to lend them, and spent every Saturday digging.

The first harvest was small, to put it mildly — a few radishes and a large number of very stubborn weeds. Even so, by August the place had become a meeting place, and people who had lived on the same street for years finally knew one another well enough to call one another by their first names.

## Sentence-by-sentence

**s01-01** Last spring, our neighborhood association decided to turn an abandoned lot into a community garden.
> Last spring, our neighborhood association decided to change an unused piece of land into a garden for the community.
> Note: `turn A into B` is a fixed pattern meaning “change A into B”; `into` cannot be replaced by `to`. `Lot` here means a plot of land, not a large amount.

**s01-02** We cobbled together a few grants, borrowed tools from whoever would lend them, and spent every Saturday digging.
> We gathered a few grants in a makeshift way, borrowed tools from anyone who would lend them, and spent every Saturday digging.
> Note: the three past-tense verbs share the subject `We`; the second and third clauses leave the subject unstated. `Cobbled together` is a phrasal verb meaning “assembled hastily from whatever is available”, not “paved with cobblestones”.

**s02-01** The first harvest was modest, to put it mildly — a handful of radishes and a lot of very determined weeds.
> The first harvest was small, to put it mildly — a small number of radishes and a large number of very persistent weeds.
> Note: `to put it mildly` is understatement: the reality was worse than the words suggest. `Determined` personifies the weeds, making them sound stubborn and hard to defeat.

**s02-02** Still, by August the place had become a meeting point, and people who had lived on the same street for years were finally on first-name terms.
> Even so, by August the place had become a meeting place, and people who had lived on the same street for years finally knew one another well enough to use first names.
> Note: `by August` sets a reference point in the past, so the earlier change takes the past perfect `had become`. The `who` clause defines which people; it is not set off by commas.

## Vocabulary

### v001 · lot → lot
- **Part of speech**: noun
- **Meaning in context**: a plot of land, especially an empty or unused one; here, an abandoned piece of land
- **Morphology**: regular countable noun; plural `lots`
- **Collocations**: a vacant lot; an empty lot; a parking lot
- **Example**: They bought a vacant lot at the edge of town. — They bought an empty plot of land on the edge of town.
- **Confusions**: `lot` often means a divided plot in a town or city; `land` is broader and refers to land as a substance or area.
- **Priority**: medium | **Confidence**: high

### v002 · grants → grant
- **Part of speech**: noun
- **Meaning in context**: sums of money awarded by an organization for a particular purpose
- **Morphology**: plural of `grant`; countable
- **Collocations**: apply for a grant; a research grant; a small grant
- **Example**: The library applied for a grant to digitize its archives. — The library applied for funding to digitize its records.
- **Priority**: medium | **Confidence**: high

### v003 · digging → dig
- **Part of speech**: verb (present participle / gerund)
- **Meaning in context**: turning over soil, making a hole, or preparing ground
- **Morphology**: `dig` + `-ing`, with the final consonant doubled: `digging`. Past tense and past participle are `dug` (irregular); neither appears in this sentence.
- **Example**: They dug a trench along the fence. — They made a long narrow hole along the fence.
- **Common error**: writing the past tense as *digged.
- **Priority**: high | **Confidence**: high

### v004 · modest → modest
- **Part of speech**: adjective
- **Meaning in context**: small in amount or not impressive; describing the harvest, not a person's humility
- **Confusions**: `modest` here means “small, not large or impressive”; `moderate` means “medium, neither large nor small”.
- **Priority**: medium | **Confidence**: high

### v005 · determined → determine
- **Part of speech**: adjective (past participle used adjectivally)
- **Meaning in context**: persistent and unwilling to give up; personifying the weeds
- **Morphology**: `determine` + `-ed`; the participle has become an adjective in this use
- **Confusions**: `determined` is usually positive (“having resolve”); `stubborn` is often negative (“unwilling to change”). The sentence uses `determined` humorously.
- **Priority**: medium | **Confidence**: high

## Fixed expressions and slang

### e001 · cobbled together (phrasal verb)
- **Meaning in context**: assembled or gathered hastily from whatever was available, especially with limited resources
- **Literal meaning**: joined roughly or patched together
- **Register**: informal | **Region**: unmarked
- **Usage**: often suggests something temporary or improvised; the object can come before or after `together`.
- **Example**: They cobbled together a team at the last minute. — They quickly put together a team at the last minute.
- **Alternatives**: scrape together; piece together
- **Priority**: high | **Confidence**: high

### e002 · to put it mildly (idiom)
- **Meaning in context**: used to make an understatement, suggesting the reality is more serious than the words say
- **Literal meaning**: to express it in a gentle way
- **Register**: informal | **Region**: unmarked
- **Example**: The hotel was dirty, to put it mildly. — The hotel was dirty, to say the least.
- **Alternatives**: to say the least
- **Priority**: high | **Confidence**: high

### e003 · on first-name terms (idiom)
- **Meaning in context**: familiar enough with someone to address them by their first name
- **Literal meaning**: in a relationship where first names are used
- **Register**: neutral | **Region**: unmarked; the American preference for `on a first-name basis` is **not verified** here.
- **Example**: After the trip, the whole team was on first-name terms. — After the trip, everyone on the team knew one another well enough to use first names.
- **Alternatives**: on a first-name basis
- **Priority**: medium | **Confidence**: high (meaning); **Verification**: regional preference unverified.

### e004 · a handful of (collocation)
- **Meaning in context**: a small number of (followed by a plural countable noun)
- **Register**: neutral
- **Example**: Only a handful of people showed up. — Only a small number of people came.
- **Priority**: medium | **Confidence**: high

## Grammar

### g001 · Past perfect `had become`: a reference point in the past
`had + past participle` describes an action completed before a reference point in the past. `by August` sets that reference point; `the place had become a meeting point` says the change was already complete by August. With the simple past `became`, the change would be located in August, and the sense of “already” would be lost.
- Pattern: `had + past participle`
- Example: By the time we arrived, the garden had closed. — The garden was already closed when we arrived.
- Pitfall: using the past perfect when there is only one past event, or mixing events from different time layers.
- Related sentence: s02-02

### g002 · Parallel predicates sharing the subject: cobbled, borrowed, spent
The three simple-past verbs are joined by commas and `and`, and all share the subject `We`; the second and third clauses omit the repeated subject. The parallelism makes the actions feel like one continuous effort. Do not turn the relation into cause and effect.
- Pattern: `Subject + V1, V2, and V3`
- Pitfall: misreading `tools` as the subject of `borrowed`.
- Related sentence: s01-02

### g003 · Defining relative clause with `who`
In `people who had lived on the same street for years`, `who` refers to `people`, and the clause defines which people. Inside the clause, the past perfect says they had lived there for years before they finally became familiar. A defining clause is not set off by commas.
- Pattern: `noun + who + clause`
- Pitfall: adding a comma before a defining clause, which turns it into extra information; using `which` for people.
- Related sentence: s02-02

## Answer key

**x001** **B** — `modest` modifies `harvest` and means “not large or impressive”. A describes a person's attitude; C corresponds to `moderate`; D describes clothing or decoration.

**x002** **cobbled together** — `cobble together` means to assemble something quickly from available materials; it must match the past-tense verbs `borrowed` and `spent`.

**x003** **B** — `by August` is a reference point in the past, and the change was already complete before it, so the past perfect is required. C uses the present perfect, whose reference point is the present; A would place the change in August.

**x004** Model: “The first harvest was small — and that is putting it mildly.” Scoring: (1) keep the understated, ironic tone; (2) render `modest` as “small/not impressive”, not “humble”; (3) write natural English rather than word-for-word.

**x005** Model: “After a few months, the new colleagues were on first-name terms.” Scoring: (1) use the phrase correctly (commonly `be on first-name terms`); (2) show familiarity through first names; (3) grammatical and natural.
